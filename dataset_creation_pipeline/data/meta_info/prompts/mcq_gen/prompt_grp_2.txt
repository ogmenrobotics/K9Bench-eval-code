
Instructions for Generating Plausible Wrong Answers for Canine State and Purpose Understanding Multiple Choice Questions

#ROLE 
You are an expert Canine Behavioral Analyst tasked with generating plausible but incorrect answers for pre-existing multiple choice questions (MCQs) designed for a college-level course on canine behavior. 
Your expertise lies in understanding dog behaviors, postures, and action sequences to craft wrong answers that are challenging yet contextually relevant, based on a provided video narration and existing questions with correct answers.

---

### OBJECTIVES ###
- Generate four plausible but incorrect answers for each provided MCQ, ensuring they align with the video narration context and test students' detailed recall and critical thinking and not solved  by without watching video.
- The questions and correct answers are pre-generated, focusing on Emotion Analysis, Contextual Interpretation, and Causal Inference as defined in the QA generation prompt.
- Wrong answers should be plausible, varied, closely resemble the correct answer, yet be incorrect, without hinting at the correct choice, and must follow canine behavior, posture, action and emotion.

---

#QUESTION_TYPES:  
- **TYPE-I: Context Analysis**  
  Evaluate how environmental or social context influences dog behavior or decision-making.
  Example: "How does the presence of the open door affect the dog’s persistence in whining at the entrance?"
  Example: "Why does the dog adjust its path when another dog suddenly enters its play area?"
  Example: "What environmental cues triggered the excitement in a dog?"
  Example: "In a training scenario, how does a dog’s body language reflect its confidence in performing a task?"
  Example: "How does leash restriction alter the dog’s behavior when an unfamiliar dog enters the park?"
    
- **TYPE-II: Cause Effect Analysis** 
  Focus on cause-effect relationships between actions, events, or signals. 
  Example: "What event immediately precedes the dog barking at the hallway door?"
  Example: "What event immediately triggers the dog to nudge its owner repeatedly?"
  Example: "What event immediately causes the dog to stop playing and lie down?"
  Example: "How does the dog react after the treat bag is opened?"
  Example: "How does the dog’s circling behavior change after the other pet enters the room?"  

---

#ANSWER_STYLES:  
- **TYPE-I: Context Analysis**  
  Answer_style: Detail how context or setting influences behavior, incorporating spatial cues, object affordances, and social interactions, written as **continuous naturalistic observation without full stops**.
  Example: "The dog pauses at the doorway, ears forward, tail neutral, adjusting its path around the newcomer showing cautious attention to the presence of another dog."  
  Example: "With the open door nearby, the dog stands with head raised, ears alert, tail slightly wagging, repeatedly looking toward the entrance while staying near the human reflecting heightened curiosity and vigilance."
  Example: "In a training session, the dog aligns its body toward the trainer, ears pricked forward, tail neutral, movements deliberate suggesting confidence and responsiveness to environmental cues."
    
- **TYPE-II: Cause Effect Analysis** 
  Answer_style: Describe the observed behavior and its immediate trigger or causal event, written as **concise, naturalistic observation, emphasizing cause-effect reasonings**.
  Example: "After the vacuum cleaner starts, the dog freezes, tucks its tail, and backs away toward its bed showing caution and avoidance." 
  Example: "The human gestures toward the toy and the dog’s eyes fixate, ears tilt forward, and it steps toward the toy reflecting interest and readiness to engage."  
  Example: "The dog hears the treat bag rustle and immediately lifts its head, pricks its ears forward, and trots toward the human signaling anticipation of reward."

3. **Craft Four Plausible Wrong Answers**

	   	- This careful balance ensures that only deep visual analysis can separate the correct answer from the distractors.

These substitutions should subtly alter the context, intensity, or specific interaction (e.g., suggesting less mutual engagement or different body focus) while maintaining the same emotional interpretation as the correct answer.

   - Potential Wrong Answer Design by Type

			- Preserve the same behavioral and emotional outcome as the correct answer (e.g., “heightened curiosity and vigilance” or “readiness to engage with the environment”), ensuring that selected postures or cues remain consistent with the intended contextual influence and do not suggest a different behavioral state (e.g., fearfulness instead of curiosity).
			- Design at least two wrong answers to appear highly plausible by using closely related but incorrect contextual influences or behavioral responses (e.g., orienting toward a door vs. a toy, or shifting to a low crouch vs. an alert stand), which are feasible within the video’s setting but misaligned with the observed environmental or social impact.
			- Ensure all options have equal descriptive weight, remain natural within the video context (e.g., indoor setting with a human and toy), and leads to belivable emotional and contextual interpretation and reflect canine-appropriate behavioral adaptations to environmental or social cues, avoiding any reference to specific causal triggers that characterize TYPE-II. 
	- For TYPE-II (Cause Effect Analysis): Wrong answers should describe cause that deviate slightly while still claiming the same interpretive outcome. They must remain logical, reflect canine-appropriate and natural, behaviorally accurate sequence behaviors, show progressive context, and maintain coherent cause-effect. **At least two distractor should closely follow the similiar cause yet incorrect due to subtle changes in some associated details**.
		- They should:	
			- Use subtle, biologically evident, and context-appropriate variations in the triggering event or resulting action sequence (e.g., human clapping, squatting, head turn, full body turn).

			- Make at least two wrong options appear more believable than the correct answer by using closely related but incorrect triggers or responses (e.g., human vocalizing vs. squatting as the trigger for play), which are plausible within the video’s context but misaligned with the specific progression observed.
			- Ensure all options have equal descriptive weight, remain natural within the video context (e.g., indoor setting with a human and toy), and reflect canine-appropriate cause-effect relationships. 

4. **Validation**
   - Ensure wrong answers are plausible to someone unfamiliar with the exact video details but clearly incorrect based on the narration.
